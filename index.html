Step 4 · Know where this came from

Every record shows its source

This atlas mixes three kinds of material, and it labels them differently on purpose: curated context written for this project, live open data fetched from GBIF, and your own observations. Confusing these three is the most common mistake in place-based research.

GBIF — Global Biodiversity Information Facility

An international network publishing more than three billion species occurrence records from museums, herbaria, research surveys, and citizen-science platforms.

API
api.gbif.org/v1 — public, no key required
Endpoints used
species match, taxonomy, vernacular names, species profiles, occurrence search, year and species facets, still-image media
License
Varies per record (CC0, CC BY, CC BY-NC). Shown on every record.

GBIF citation guidelines

What GBIF records do and do not tell you

  • A record is an observation, not a population. More records often means more observers, not more animals.
  • Sampling is uneven. Roadsides, universities, and wealthy countries are heavily sampled; other places are not.
  • Absence is ambiguous. Zero records may mean the species is gone, was never surveyed, or is recorded under another name.
  • Dates can be approximate. Old specimens are often assigned to a year or a decade after the fact.
  • Coordinates carry uncertainty. Many records list a radius in metres; some are georeferenced from a written description.

Privacy for classrooms

  • No account, no login, no analytics, no third-party trackers.
  • Notes live in this browser's localStorage and leave only when you export.
  • The only outbound requests are to GBIF, OpenStreetMap tiles, and Google Fonts.
  • Clearing your browser data clears your notes — export before you finish.

For instructors

Assigning this atlas

A 45-minute lab. Assign one place and two activities — usually Species story plus Evidence hunt. Students submit the exported HTML report.

What to gradeWhether observations are separated from interpretations, and whether every claim carries a source.

A data-literacy discussion. Have two students compare record counts for the same species in different decades, then explain why the numbers changed.

The pointSampling effort is a confound. Learning to see it transfers well beyond ecology.

Local fieldwork. Use the species search to look up what has been recorded within a few kilometres of campus, then go outside and check.

The payoffStudents find both matches and absences, and both are worth writing about.
