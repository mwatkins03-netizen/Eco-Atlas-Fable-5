The idea

An ecography is a biography of a place

A biography tells the story of a person. An ecography tells the story of a place and everything living in it — what is there, how it got there, who noticed, and who was never asked. You are the author. The data is your evidence, not your conclusion.

You will be working with real records. Every species you see was recorded by a specific person or institution at a specific place and time, and published through GBIF — the Global Biodiversity Information Facility. Museums, university surveys, bird counts, and people with phones all feed the same database.

That means the data has a point of view. Part of your job is to notice whose.

Your circle can be anywhere. Your campus. The block you grew up on. A river you fish. A park you have walked past a thousand times without looking.

The most interesting ecographies are usually about ordinary places the author already knows — because you can check the data against your own eyes.

Why it matters

The data will not tell you what lives there

It will tell you what somebody wrote down. Those are different, and the gap between them is where the good questions live.

  • More records usually means more observers, not more animals. City parks look richer than remote forest because more people with phones walk through them.
  • Absence is ambiguous. Zero records might mean gone, never surveyed, never digitized, or filed under a name you did not search.
  • Seasons skew everything. Most records come from spring and summer, when it is pleasant to be outside looking.
  • Some knowledge never enters a database. What a farmer knows about a field, or what a family has watched happen to a creek over forty years, is real evidence and no occurrence record contains it.
